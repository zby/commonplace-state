# Premise decomposition: What bound Naur's theory to programmers

**Note:** kb/articles/what-bound-naurs-theory-to-programmers.md
**Register:** claim
**Central commitment (one sentence):** Read with "formulated criteria" meaning a human-statable rubric, Naur's argument for binding program theory to humans depends on a bridge premise that trained recognizers defeat, so that route to the human-only conclusion is blocked without showing that any machine or composite holds a program's theory.

## Premises and counterexamples
1. **Universal (undeclared): in the strand of Naur's argument this article targets, the human-only conclusion depends on the bridge premise that machines judge only by formulated criteria, rather than following independently from his other human-centered passages.** — DOUBTFUL — The article itself quotes passages about relevance being decidable only by someone who understands the whole world and theory being a programmer's mental possession; those can be read as an independent human-binding route even if the bridge premise fails. — GLOBAL — instance
2. **Universal (undeclared): here "formulated criteria" means a rubric a person could state and apply, not any finite mechanism that fully determines an output.** — DOUBTFUL — A broader reading can treat a trained recognizer's architecture, weights, and threshold as the determining criteria, which keeps the recognizer on Naur's machine side and preserves the bridge. — GLOBAL — instance
3. **Bounded to the programs Naur describes: for the compiler and production-monitoring cases he discusses, making a machine judge did require criteria programmers had explicitly formulated.** — HOLDS — The active hunt found no contrary case inside the programs the article names, and the article explicitly narrows this premise to those cases.
4. **Existential: at least one trained recognizer performs the sort of similarity judgment Naur uses as his example while no person can state the operative rubric, yet the recognizer still runs as formal symbol manipulation on a computer.** — HOLDS — Face recognition is the article's concrete witness, and Naur's own examples include faces and tunes; the active hunt found no reason to deny that such systems output that kind of similarity judgment without a human-usable rubric.
5. **Universal (undeclared): if the bridge premise is universal, one machine counterinstance without a human-statable rubric is enough to block that step even though it does not establish theory possession.** — HOLDS — If the bridge says machine judgment requires formulated criteria, one genuine counterinstance defeats that bridge while leaving the stronger bearer question open.
6. **Universal (undeclared): Naur's bearer requirements of world-mapping, part-justification, and coherent extension remain distinct requirements that are not met merely because a machine can output a similarity judgment.** — HOLDS — The active hunt found no reason to collapse those bearer requirements into similarity output alone; the article's narrow result depends on keeping them separate.
7. **Bounded case claim: the project's automated repair episode is evidence of a composite failing Naur's coherent-modification requirement, not merely a narrower defect in prompting, retrieval, or workflow setup.** — DOUBTFUL — The episode also admits a more local reading: the pass had the note and review outputs but not the one reconstructed premise the maintainer later supplied, so the failure may reflect missing scoped guidance rather than a clean demonstration that no text-plus-interpreter composite there held the note's theory. — LOCAL — instance

## For the human
Look first at premise 1: if Naur's world-understanding and mental-possession passages already bind theory to humans on their own, defeating the machine/formulated-criteria bridge does not reopen the human-only conclusion.
