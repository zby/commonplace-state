kb/sources/requirements-reflection-runtime-entities.ingest.md:16:# Ingest: Requirements Reflection: Requirements as Runtime Entities
kb/sources/requirements-reflection-runtime-entities.ingest.md:20:This is an ICSE 2010 scientific vision and design paper: it defines requirements reflection, sketches a two-stratum runtime architecture, and states research challenges rather than reporting a realized system or controlled evaluation. Author: researchers at Lancaster University and University College London working in requirements engineering, reflective middleware, goal modeling, and self-adaptive systems; the paper locates its proposal within those research literatures and the authors' related modeling work.
kb/sources/requirements-reflection-runtime-entities.ingest.md:24:The paper argues that requirements monitoring leaves a running system with low-level derived artifacts but not the high-level goals, assumptions, alternatives, and relationships that motivated them. It proposes reifying those requirements as first-class runtime entities in a goal-oriented meta-model, exposing operations for inspecting and changing them, and semantically synchronizing that requirements stratum with a reflective architecture stratum so changes on either side can affect or be checked against the other. It also proposes runtime reasoning about uncertainty and interactive multi-objective trade-offs. Read it as a concrete structural agenda for putting a software system's purposes on a causal path to adaptation, not as evidence that the architecture works or that the system learns its own requirements model.
kb/sources/requirements-reflection-runtime-entities.ingest.md:40:5. **The evidence supports historical positioning, not effectiveness** -- The paper offers a motivating vacuum-cleaner scenario, conceptual comparison with prior work, and a research agenda, but no implementation, benchmark, ablation, or outcome data. It can ground a lineage claim and sharpen a baseline; it cannot show that requirements reflection improves adaptation, explanation, safety, or learning. [just-a-reference]

kb/sources/awareness-requirements-adaptive-systems.ingest.md:17:# Ingest: Awareness Requirements for Adaptive Systems
kb/sources/awareness-requirements-adaptive-systems.ingest.md:25:Awareness Requirements (AwReqs) specify constraints on the runtime states of other requirements or domain assumptions: never fail, succeed at a specified rate over a time window, avoid declining success rates, or complete within a time bound. They can also refer to other AwReqs in successive strata. The paper connects stakeholder goals and qualitative softgoals to measurable quality constraints, then to requirements about their observed satisfaction. Requirements become monitor-side UML classes and runtime instances, with temporal constraints expressed in OCL with Temporal Message logic and compiled by EEAT into event-processing rules. Constructed scenarios reportedly yield the expected satisfaction verdicts within this instrumented class-and-event representation; they do not compare alternative goal decompositions or demonstrate successful end-to-end adaptation. Compensation, diagnosis and systematic construction of a complete monitor-analyze-plan-execute loop remain future work.
kb/sources/awareness-requirements-adaptive-systems.ingest.md:39:It is also a boundary case for [Diagnostic richness constrains outer-loop learning quality](../theory/diagnostic-richness-constrains-outer-loop-learning-quality.md). AwReqs identify violated constraints and aggregate their histories; they do not by themselves explain a violation or choose a repair. The paper thus supplies a concrete monitoring mechanism to compare with diagnostic evidence, not evidence that satisfaction signals alone support reliable knowledge revision.
kb/sources/awareness-requirements-adaptive-systems.ingest.md:43:The proposed adaptation mechanism is a feedback loop driven by violations of explicit requirements about other requirements. Only its monitoring stage is implemented here. Meta-AwReq examples suggest escalation, changing success-rate targets, or temporarily disabling a requirement to avoid repeated notifications. These are illustrations of possible reconciliation policies, not evaluated learning algorithms. The paper's “awareness” means reference to requirement states, not consciousness; its goal decomposition is not itself Commonplace's technical concept of program refinement.
kb/sources/awareness-requirements-adaptive-systems.ingest.md:52:2. **Separate authority to change a target from detection of failure.** The meta-AwReq example reduces response-time success targets to suppress repeated notifications. For agent-operated knowledge work, this illustrates why an alert-control policy must not silently become authority to redefine substantive success. The paper illustrates the change but does not establish its legitimacy or benefit. [quick-win]
kb/sources/awareness-requirements-adaptive-systems.ingest.md:58:The central empirical support is correct evaluation of authored scenarios, including true, false and indeterminate cases for selected patterns. This tests implementation against expected monitor verdicts, not improved ambulance-dispatch outcomes, usability of elicitation, or superiority to competing formalisms. The claims that AwReqs are simpler or more accessible than fuzzy alternatives lack a comparative stakeholder study here.

A separate semantic mapping must propagate requirements changes into architecture and check architecture changes against requirements, so storing or querying a goal model alone does not realize requirements reflection.
