# Critique: A fixed review rule can use lessons from new incidents only if it can identify when they apply

**Note:** kb/theory/fixed-review-rules-must-identify-when-incident-lessons-apply.md
**Central commitment:** A standing instruction such as "check past incidents before publication" can stay fixed and still use lessons from new incidents, because the real test is whether the reviewer recognizes the structural feature of an earlier failure on a new page, not whether the rule changes.
**Critique mode:** claim
**Attack outcome:** partially lands

## Strongest case against it

The opponent advocates codification. Aviation and surgical checklist practice is the model, together with research on analogical transfer, such as Gick and Holyoak's finding that people rarely apply a structurally analogous solution spontaneously unless prompted to look for it. The argument runs as follows.

"Check past incidents" sends the reviewer to an archive. The note admits that looking up incidents by surface features, such as source names, misses the relevant connection. What remains is the reviewer's unprompted recognition that the new page has the same deep structure: citations that look independent but share an origin, supporting a claim that depends on their independence. Recognition across different surface details is exactly the kind that fails in practice. It fails more often as the archive grows and as review becomes routine. A lesson that is reliably applied therefore has to be turned into an applicability condition that someone tells the reviewer: "when a figure rests on several citations offered as independent, check whether they share an origin." Either the rule changes to say this, or the incident record is rewritten from a story into that condition. In both cases, something the reviewer is told must change with each new kind of lesson. "The rule need not change" is true only of the rule's wording. It is false of the instruction surface the reviewer actually depends on.

The attack also points to an internal tension. The title says the rule must "identify when they apply". The body says the rule cannot do that, and the description says it "leaves the reviewer to determine" it. On the note's own account, the rule does not meet the title's condition, so the title implies that the rule cannot use the lessons. That reading supports the opponent.

## How the note engages it

Partially engaged. The note concedes the surface-retrieval failure ("A lookup by source name would miss that connection") and correctly moves the success test from retrieval to discrimination ("whether the reviewer distinguishes apparent corroboration from independent support"). That is a real answer to a naive objection that incidents just need better indexing.

The note does not engage the reliability question. Its only support for the unchanged rule working is "A reviewer following the original instruction could still recognize it". That establishes possibility, while the opponent's case concerns dependability. The note also never says where the distinction the incident teaches is recorded once the rule stays fixed. Its last sentence ("not that the review rule must change") rejects one location without naming another. The opponent's claim, that an applicability condition must be written somewhere the reviewer encounters, survives. The note's narrower claim also survives: the rule's wording need not change if the incident record or the reviewer carries the condition. Hence the outcome is partially lands, not lands.

## Constructive findings

- Name what supplies recognition when the rule stays fixed. One option is incident records written as applicability conditions rather than narratives. The other is reviewer competence that is stated as a requirement. State the cost of each.
- Replace "could still recognize it" with the condition under which recognition is dependable, or limit the claim to possibility and say that reliability needs a further mechanism.
- Make the title's subject the thing that actually identifies applicability, the reviewer or the incident record, not the rule. The title, description and body should then make one claim.
- Raise the final sentence's contestable claim, that a fixed rule plus a changing record of distinctions is enough, into the title or description, so that the "only if" is not read as a tautology.

## Secondary objections (optional)

- The single wiki example uses an unusually crisp structural feature (shared origin under an independence claim). Lessons from incidents whose relevant feature is vaguer may not reduce to one distinction a reviewer can check. The note does not say whether its claim covers those cases.
- The note does not consider review load. A fixed rule that sends the reviewer to an ever-growing archive raises the cost of each review even when recognition succeeds. That cost may justify changing the rule, for example by selecting which incidents to consult, for reasons other than applicability.
