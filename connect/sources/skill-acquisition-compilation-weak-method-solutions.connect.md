## Flags

This is ONR-85-1 dated August 12, 1985, a technical-report version rather than an assumed byte-equivalent 1987 journal article. OCR is noisy, particularly formulas, LISP punctuation and diagrams. The body and appendix provide the usable mechanism; do not reconstruct exact executable rules from damaged text. No implementation was inspected or executed. Appendix GRAPES formalism is a described symbolic simulation, not an LLM or modern agent runtime.

The account uses condition–action productions, hierarchical goals, declarative knowledge interpreted through supplied weak methods, proceduralization that removes declarative lookups, composition that combines goal-achieving sequences, and strengthening through successful use. Goals determine the segment to compile. The declarative/procedural distinction is a functional one here, not identical to Commonplace's natural-language/symbolic form boundary. General weak methods are assumed givens; learned rules do not establish that the supplied representation or compiler is adequate for every domain.

The initial FIRST/SECOND example combines a detailed individual protocol and simulation with tutor observations from 38 subjects. It reports large first-to-second improvements, but a fitted simulation does not uniquely establish a human mechanism. Text-editor transfer predictions use independently motivated production analyses to reduce post-hoc fitting; near-total ED/EDT transfer contrasts with limited execution transfer to EMACS. Perverse-EMACS results preserve substantial transfer despite changed key bindings, not proof that all procedural learning is interference-free. The author distinguishes maladaptive use of a successfully transferred procedure from impaired firing of that procedure.

Function-pair frequency is counterbalanced while individual-function frequency is held fixed; reported evaluation means are 6.55 versus 8.61 seconds. Evaluation practice improves time 18.2 to 10.0 seconds and accuracy 58 to 85 percent, while generation changes 71 to 74 percent. The separate proof-reason-giving example is explicitly limited by a small sample and informal assessment. Several studies are summarized without full sample sizes, uncertainty estimates or methods, and some references were in preparation. These are reported human-learning findings, not fresh replications or direct agent evidence.

Working-memory load can produce omissions and wrong-rule intrusions even with a correct rule set. Immediate feedback is proposed to preserve decision-point information for correct compilation; giving away answers can instead train copying. Discovery improves recognition of error states, and debugging practice can justify allowing errors, so immediate feedback is not claimed best for every learning target. How-to recursion instruction and in-context tutor interventions improve reported speed and exam scores, but the source acknowledges the tutor's cognitive model as a prerequisite.

The report revises earlier ACT assumptions: automatic syntactic generalization/discrimination are replaced by strategic problem solving informed by semantic knowledge and then compiled. Single-example generalization can therefore rely on prior understanding, not induction from one datum alone. The source's ambition to cover all cognitive skill acquisition exceeds the bounded adult technical-skill evidence. Transfer to children, domains beyond those tested, or modern agent memory requires additional evidence.
