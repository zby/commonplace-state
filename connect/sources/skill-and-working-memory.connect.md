## Flags

The core longitudinal evidence comes from two trained runners, SF and DD: maximum digit spans of 82 and 68 after hundreds of sessions, with semantic associations to running times, ages, years and patterns. SF's simulation predicts reported coding choices at about 90 percent, but fitting verbal protocols does not uniquely identify the full memory mechanism. Early manipulations of codeable versus uncodeable digits alter span, and semantic post-session recall and recognition show durable retention. The theorized ingredients are meaningful encoding, a learned hierarchical retrieval structure indexing order/locations, and faster encoding/retrieval with practice.

Alphabet recitation primarily disrupts the final rehearsal group; visual suppression and repetitive chanting have little effect, while interleaved letter shadowing reduces performance about 35 percent. Digit-group size, rehearsal limits and unchanged consonant span support the narrower inference that practice did not expand general short-term capacity. One participant with mnemonic codes but no developed retrieval structure plateaued near eighteen digits, a suggestive individual contrast rather than a randomized component ablation. SF could also use the structure for fourteen names; absence of letter-span transfer is not proof of absolute representational exclusivity.

Hierarchy-boundary retrieval costs, pause patterns and retrospective reports support structured retrieval. Some speed evidence is explicitly limited and DD's practice trend is less clear than SF's. When given enough learning time, unskilled subjects can approach experts' retrieval times on the learned matrix. At three digits per second, even trained subjects achieve far less than their ordinary maximum span. Useful capacity therefore depends on encoding time and task conditions, not just stored volume.

The proposed memory trace binds semantic features, location cues and context. Semantic code narrows a search but need not uniquely reconstruct the original digits. Redundant relations and differentiation help disambiguation; reused categories and locations create interference. Later trial items can be poor in ordered recall yet good in post-session semantic recall. Some forgetting alternatives remain unresolved; hypothesized links and feature loss are explanations, not directly observed neural structure.

The mental-calculation case uses one expert, verbal protocols and fitted latency/error models. Mnemonics retain remote partial products while an iterative execution minimizes memory load despite recursive mathematical structure. The waiter case is explicitly unfinished and uses simulated random orders, a harder distribution than the workplace; the number of items also changes across sessions. Sentence studies compare meaningful versus scrambled sequences, roughly fourteen versus six-to-seven-word span, and show strong cued-recall benefits. They broaden the phenomenon beyond exceptional experts but do not establish universal training gains. The conclusion expands working memory functionally to include task-specific long-term retrieval and context; its mechanistic theory remains tentative.
