# Friction check: What bound Naur's theory to programmers

**Note:** kb/articles/what-bound-naurs-theory-to-programmers.md
**Central claim (one sentence):** On the article's reading of Naur, his argument for binding program theory to humans depends on a bridge premise that machine judgment requires formulated criteria; trained recognizers block that premise without showing that any present machine-plus-text composite holds a program's theory, and the project's repair episode is one bounded case of such a composite failing Naur's coherent-extension test.

## Filter
**Verdict:** SURVIVES
The claim wants four things to hold at once: that Naur's human-binding argument turns on a specific bridge premise, that this premise fit the programs he described, that trained recognizers later break that bridge, and that breaking it reopens rather than settles the bearer question. Those commitments can coexist. Losing an exclusion step does not imply machine success, and one failed composite does not restore human exclusivity.

## Signal — thinnest joints
1. **“This article takes the first reading [of ‘formulated’] for two reasons in Naur's text.”** — THIN — The article makes the human-usable-rubric reading plausible, but it does not establish decisively that Naur meant to exclude every fully determining learned mechanism from “criteria.” The bridge diagnosis still rests on an interpretation that needs firmer textual anchoring.
2. **“The second step is the bridge. Naur does not argue it because he did not need to for the compiler and production-monitoring programs he describes.”** — THIN — The article shows that the bridge fits those examples, but it does not fully rule out nearby passages about mental possession and understanding “the whole world” as independent routes to human binding. Those rival routes are acknowledged, not decisively discharged.
3. **“A face recognizer is formal symbol manipulation by a program, yet it performs a similarity judgment for which no person can state the rubric.”** — THIN — This is a good witness against the generalization that machine judgment always needs a human-stated rubric, but it only blocks the article's target if the same notion of formulated criteria is doing the argumentative work in Naur. The shared term still needs tighter pinning.
4. **“A language model judging whether a new demand resembles what a design already provides is formally on the recognizer's side of that line.”** — UNSUPPORTED — The face-recognizer witness establishes opaque discrimination, not the world-directed, program-specific, reason-responsive similarity judgment Naur's modification test requires. The article later marks that gap, but this analogy still imports too much from the recognizer case.
5. **“The purpose was available but did not guide the repair, which makes this a failure of use rather than simple retrieval.”** — THIN — The repair episode does show a bounded inability to preserve the note's governing direction, but it does not isolate theory-possession failure from alternatives such as optimization toward defensibility, underspecified purpose constraints, or local repair-policy effects.

## For the human
Look first at the jump from the face-recognizer witness to the language-model design-judgment analogy; that is the thinnest joint carrying the article's reopened question.
