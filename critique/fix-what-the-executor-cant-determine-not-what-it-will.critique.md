# Critique: An instruction should fix what the executor can't determine, not what it will

**Note:** kb/notes/fix-what-the-executor-cant-determine-not-what-it-will.md
**Central commitment:** The right line for what an instruction should pin down is a *visibility* cut — fix the goal, constraints, done-condition, and arbitrary-but-consistent conventions the executor can't reach; leave everything the executor will see for itself to the executor, because a fixed detail is an authoring-time snapshot that goes stale by ignorance or drift.
**Critique mode:** claim

## Strongest case against it

The opposing stance is the **reliability-engineering / method-codification position** — the view behind detailed runbooks, SRE playbooks, ISO-9001 work instructions, regulated SOPs, and the prompt-engineering practice of pinning interpretation with explicit steps. Call its proponent the *procedure engineer*. Their claim: **the right line for what an instruction fixes is set by the cost of variance, not by who can see the situation.** They would say the note has mislocated the cut.

Their best reasoning, in four moves:

1. **The visibility cut is not the operative variable; the variance-vs-stake tradeoff is.** The note says: if the executor will see it, leave it to the executor. The engineer answers: *visibility does not imply correct choice.* A competent executor that *can* see the situation still resolves an [underspecified instruction by projection](../../../notes/agentic-systems-interpret-underspecified-instructions.md) — it picks *one* of many valid interpretations, and you can't predict which. Seeing the situation removes the *ignorance* of the detail; it does nothing about the *plurality of valid responses* to what is seen. So "leave it to the executor because the executor will see it" is a non-sequitur whenever the task admits multiple acceptable resolutions and you need a *specific, repeatable* one. The note's own neighbor note establishes exactly this — and the note never reconciles with it beyond a one-line "contrasts" link that frames under-specification as merely a "dual failure," as if the two errors lived on opposite ends of one dial with a sweet spot in the middle. They don't: a detail can be simultaneously visible-to-the-executor *and* one the author must fix, because the executor's freedom to see is orthogonal to its freedom to choose wrong.

2. **"Leave what the executor will see to the executor" proves too much.** Take its strongest reading literally. Almost every detail in a real procedure is something the executor *will* see at runtime: the variable name is in the file, the section to edit is on screen, the format of the output is inspectable. By the note's test, all of these should be left to the executor "so its output tracks the system." But this is precisely the recipe for inconsistent, unreviewable, drifting output — the exact failure mode runbooks exist to kill. The note tries to wall this off with the "arbitrary-but-consistent conventions" carve-out (paths, names, templates). But that carve-out is *under-theorized and does the load-bearing work*: enormously many details that need fixing are not "arbitrary" (there *is* a better answer) yet still must be pinned for consistency/reliability — e.g. "always quote the user verbatim, never paraphrase," "edit the section titled X, not whichever section currently holds the content." These are non-arbitrary (paraphrase is sometimes locally better) and fully visible to the executor, yet a good instruction fixes them. The note's two buckets — situation-dependent-so-omit, arbitrary-so-fix — have no room for the largest real category: *visible, non-arbitrary, but variance-costly, so fix anyway.*

3. **The drift argument is the ignorance argument wearing a hat.** The note presents two reasons a fixed detail goes stale: author ignorance at authoring time, and system evolution after. But evolution only bites *if the detail was a guess about a contingent state in the first place.* A goal, a constraint, a done-condition — the things the note says to fix — also "change as the system evolves," yet the note exempts them. So drift does not pick out a category of detail; it merely re-describes "this was a contingent fact the author shouldn't have frozen," which is the ignorance point restated. The drift framing adds rhetorical force ("which we *want* it to") but no independent discriminating power. Strip it out and the note's distinctive contribution shrinks to the ordinary "say what, not how," which the note itself admits is the real content.

4. **The frontloading "reconciliation" hides a genuine conflict rather than dissolving it.** The note says this cost "is not separate from frontloading — it is a term frontloading's decision has to include." But frontloading's whole constitutive argument (per the linked note) is that inserting a known value can be the difference between a reliable and an unreliable call — *even when the inserted value is something the executor could have derived.* The procedure engineer's frontloading move is exactly: pre-resolve and inline the detail *so the executor doesn't get to choose*, because removing the choice is the point. Calling robustness "a term in the same decision" papers over a real disagreement about sign: the note treats executor-derivability as a reason *against* fixing (you forfeit runtime tracking), while frontloading treats removing executor work as the *benefit*. Two notes pulling opposite directions on the same act, reconciled by asserting they're "the same decision," is not a reconciliation — it's a deferral.

The engineer's positive thesis: **fix any detail whose variation is more costly than its staleness risk, regardless of executor visibility.** Visibility is one input to staleness risk; it is not the cut.

## How the note engages it

**Partially engaged.**

- On move 1 (visibility ≠ correct choice): **unengaged in substance, gestured at structurally.** The note's two load-bearing premises (paragraph 5) are *competence* and *static-task*. Neither is the engineer's point. The engineer grants a competent executor and a moving system and *still* says fix the detail, because competence-to-see is not competence-to-pick-the-wanted-interpretation. The note never distinguishes "can perceive the situation" from "will resolve it the way the author needs." The "contrasts" link to the underspecification note names the dual but frames it as amount-on-an-axis ("the skill is picking the right axis, not the right amount"), which actually *concedes* the engineer's framing without absorbing its bite — if it's about axis not amount, then visibility (an amount-side intuition) can't be the axis either.

- On move 2 (proves too much): **partially engaged via the arbitrary-conventions paragraph**, but the engagement is incomplete. The note anticipates that *some* fixed details aren't situation-dependent and carves out "arbitrary." It does not anticipate the *non-arbitrary-but-variance-costly* category, which is where the strongest counterexamples live. The carve-out is asserted as a clean mirror image ("no one decides better") rather than defended against the case where someone *does* decide better yet consistency still demands a fixed answer.

- On move 3 (drift is idle): **unengaged.** The note treats ignorance and drift as two independent legs ("dropping either flips it" is said of the *other* pair — competence/static). It never asks whether drift adds discriminating power beyond ignorance, and in fact applies drift selectively (details drift, but goals/constraints somehow don't).

- On move 4 (frontloading conflict): **partially engaged.** The note explicitly raises the frontloading relationship and even concedes "a value can be worth inserting for context and still be wrong to fix" — which *names* the tension. But it resolves it by fiat ("both pulls belong to the same decision") without giving a rule for which pull wins when they oppose. The engineer's objection survives the engagement intact.

## Constructive findings

- **Reframe the cut as variance-cost, not visibility, OR explicitly subordinate visibility to it.** The defensible core is probably: *leave to the executor what it will see for itself AND where any of its likely resolutions is acceptable.* Adding the second conjunct directly absorbs move 1 and move 2 — it explains why "edit the section titled X" gets fixed (visible, but variant resolutions are not all acceptable) without retreating to "arbitrary."

- **Promote the missing third category.** State plainly that some visible, non-arbitrary details must still be fixed when variance is costlier than staleness. This converts the unguarded "arbitrary conventions" mirror into a genuine taxonomy and removes the "proves too much" reading.

- **Give drift independent work or merge it.** Either show a case that drift catches but ignorance doesn't (e.g. a detail the author saw *correctly* at authoring time but that the system later changed — where ignorance is genuinely absent), or fold drift into ignorance and drop the claim that there are "two reasons."

- **Supply the tie-break with frontloading.** State which pull dominates when robustness (insert-to-constrain) and tracking (omit-to-let-executor-see) conflict — e.g. "fix when the wanted resolution is narrower than the executor's acceptable-resolution space; omit when any acceptable resolution will do." Without a tie-break, "same decision" is not a reconciliation.

## Secondary objections (optional)

- **The reliability/auditability function of over-specification is unmentioned.** Detailed instructions are not only about controlling a present executor; they make runs *comparable and reviewable* across executors and time. The note evaluates a fixed detail purely by whether it goes stale, ignoring that fixing it buys a fixed referent for review even when a freer instruction would also "work."
- **"Privileged fact the author holds that the executor can't reach" quietly smuggles the counterexample.** Authorial intent about *which* of several acceptable outputs is wanted is exactly such a privileged fact — and it is omnipresent, not a corner case. If the note admits this fully, the "leave the visible to the executor" rule narrows to near-vacuity; if it doesn't, it's under-claiming.
- **Adversarial/unreliable executors.** The note's competence premise is binary, but real LLM executors are competent-on-average and unreliable-in-the-tail. Method specification is cheap insurance against the tail even for a generally competent executor; the note's "competent ⇒ help becomes harm" leg ignores tail risk.
